The race at a glance

Ron DeSantis cannot run again, so Florida elects a new governor on November 3. The Florida Division of Elections lists ten qualified tickets. The two major-party nominees are Republican Byron Donalds, a sitting member of Congress, and Democrat David Jolly, who represented Florida's 13th District from 2014 to 2017 as a Republican and joined the Democratic Party in April 2025.[[s:1]][[s:2]]

[[el:stats_gov]]

The candidates

Donalds' running mate is Bryan Avila. Jolly's is Gwen Graham. Governor is a state office, so TIP's six-issue grades, which come from recorded congressional acts, appear only where a candidate has a congressional record on file. Donalds has one; Jolly has none on file at TIP today, which is not a score of zero.

Follow the money

Three kinds of money are in play, and they cannot be added together.

Public matching funds are paid by the state to governor candidates. The Department of State's table, updated October 23, shows $3,074,954.60 paid to Jolly, $1,197,766.78 to Donalds and $199,783.22 to Jay Collins, who was eliminated in the Republican primary, for a total of $4,472,504.60.[[s:3]] These payments can already be counted inside a campaign's receipts.

Campaign accounts are the candidates' own committees. As of September 11, the News Service of Florida reported that Donalds' campaign account had raised $13.3 million and spent nearly $7.4 million, and Jolly's had raised $8.2 million and spent $4.9 million.[[s:4]]

Political committees are separate groups that back a candidate. As of the same date the News Service of Florida reported that Friends of Byron Donalds had raised $107.6 million and spent nearly $70 million, and that Florida 2026, the committee backing Jolly, had received $6.4 million and spent $3.3 million.[[s:4]] That is the largest gap in the race: committee money, not the campaign accounts, which differ by a factor of about 1.6.

We could not pull these from the state's campaign finance database, which blocks automated access, so we attribute them to the press report and date them September 11. Later filings will have moved. Donalds' committee money in particular includes transfers and cannot be read as money spent on advertising.

What the latest poll measured

The University of North Florida's Public Opinion Research Lab surveyed 1,471 likely midterm voters by telephone October 12 to 19. Donalds had 49%, Jolly 47% and 4% were undecided. The margin of error is ±2.81 points. In UNF's July poll Donalds led by five points.[[s:5]] The gap now is within about the margin of error, so the poll describes a close race but cannot say who is ahead. We did not use other October polls because we could not retrieve their original releases.

What the market says

Kalshi's Florida governor contract resolves on the party of the person inaugurated. At our capture the Republican contract's last trade was 82¢ and the Democratic contract's was 19¢.[[s:6]] That is a market-implied estimate of how traders valued a party winning. It is not a vote share, and it should not be read against a poll as if they measured the same thing. A two-point poll and an 82¢ price can both be accurate descriptions of what they are.

What each campaign says it would do

Insurance. Donalds' campaign page proposes protecting recent lawsuit reforms, trimming state fees when reserves are full, reforming My Safe Florida Home, keeping Citizens Insurance small and creating an online insurer scorecard.[[s:7]] Jolly's campaign page, dated September 17, proposes a single state-backed hurricane fund so private insurers no longer price wind risk into homeowners policies. It cites a calculator that shows a $300,000 home's premium falling from $7,136 to $2,557 without wind and hurricane coverage, and lists three funding options: taxes insurers currently avoid, a fee on real estate transactions or existing tourist taxes. It says the Legislature would write the specifics.[[s:8]] Those are the campaigns' own figures and claims.

Schools. Donalds' page proposes that education money follow the student and sets a goal of reading proficiency by third grade, with phonics training, early screening and free tutoring.[[s:9]] Jolly's page proposes dramatically higher teacher pay, funded in part by changing how the state's tourist development tax can be used, which would need a change in law.[[s:10]]

Taxes and executive power. Donalds' affordability page says he would keep property taxes in check and gives no rate or measure.[[s:7]] A governor cannot change property taxes alone; the Legislature, local governments and, for constitutional changes, voters have the power. A promise on a campaign page is not an enacted policy.

The other eight tickets

The state's list includes five no-party tickets, one Libertarian ticket and two write-in tickets. They qualified under Florida law and none has a TIP record yet.[[s:1]]
