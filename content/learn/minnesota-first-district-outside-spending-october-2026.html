A House contest drawing outside money

MPR News reported October 9 that outside group money in Minnesota’s 1st Congressional District race was now outpacing the statewide Senate race as a draw for such money. The outlet, citing Federal Election Commission data, put outside spending in the district above $2.5 million, most of it opposing Republican incumbent Brad Finstad. His challenger is Jake Johnson of the Democratic-Farmer-Labor Party (DFL), a Rochester high school math teacher.[[s:1]]

The comparison is MPR’s, and it describes a recent stretch. In its October 12 report on the Senate race, MPR put outside spending there at more than $20 million in the primary and about $2 million since, so the two accounts cover different periods.[[s:6]] It is not a claim that a House seat is more important or that the money determines its result.

This article is dated October 31, 2026. The spending accounts below were published earlier in October; the market quotation comes from an October 31 capture. They describe different moments and should retain their dates.

Tariffs and rural health care divided the debate

MPR covered the October 8 debate carried by two southern Minnesota television stations. Finstad defended President Donald Trump’s tariffs. Johnson called tariffs a tax and said he would vote to end them. Those accounts offer a concrete policy disagreement rather than a conclusion inferred from either candidate’s party.[[s:1]]

On rural health care, Finstad said the Trump administration had put $50 billion into rural health care funding, while Johnson said it had cut $137 billion from rural health care through Medicaid, as MPR reported. Both dollar amounts are the candidates’ own assertions.[[s:1]]

What the debate account establishes is that the candidates offered different descriptions of federal policy. Determining how those descriptions fit together would require the underlying legislation and comparable spending estimates.[[s:1]]

The reported spending figures stay separate

In its October 9 report, MPR said a group tied to House Majority Whip Tom Emmer had deployed $600,000 in advertisements critical of Johnson that week. MPR’s account includes both that ad buy and the more than $2.5 million in outside money, most of it opposing Finstad; it does not say whether the figures overlap.[[s:1]]

FOX 9’s October 22 account reported a separate Republican-side figure: about $400,000 recently added by GOP political action committees. It also reported that Johnson, a first-time candidate, had outspent Finstad by more than $500,000. FOX 9 did not give a common reporting cutoff or source that would let those figures be reconciled with MPR’s.[[s:2]]

The $600,000 and approximately $400,000 figures should not be added into a new Republican total. Nor should either be subtracted from the earlier outside-spending figure.

FOX 9 did not say whether the more than $500,000 gap counts candidate spending, outside spending or both.[[s:2]]

What the earlier result does and does not tell us

FOX 9 reported that Finstad won by 17 percentage points in 2024 and described this contest as one of the closest among Minnesota’s eight House races. The earlier election margin is historical context. It is not a current polling margin or evidence, by itself, of how far opinion has moved.[[s:2]]

The outlet attributed an explanation for the competitiveness to University of Minnesota political scientist Larry Jacobs, who pointed to farm affordability pressure that he linked to tariffs and the war in Iran. That is Jacobs’s interpretation, rather than a demonstrated cause established by this article. FOX 9 also referred to polling without identifying a poll, so this article uses no numerical poll from that report.[[s:2]]
