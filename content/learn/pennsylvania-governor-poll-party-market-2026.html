The race at a glance

A large lead can make the next comparison sound obvious. In the Pennsylvania governor race, Quinnipiac’s October 7, 2026 release put Josh Shapiro at 59 percent and Stacy Garrity at 34 percent among likely voters. But the pollster warned against comparing that survey with its earlier registered-voter polls.[[s:1]]

That warning matters as of November 1, 2026. The October survey and a separately dated Kalshi quotation describe different things: responses from likely voters and prices for contracts that resolve on a party. They do not supply an election result or an explanation for one.[[s:1]][[s:2]]

Who is in the field

The roster transcribed from The Green Papers lists incumbent Democrat Shapiro, Republican Garrity and Libertarian Ken Krawchuk. It was captured on November 1, 2026.[[s:3]]

The election is November 3, 2026. Readers can open the linked race and candidate records on The Issues Party for the people discussed here. The polling figures below describe the survey’s respondents, rather than a count of ballots cast.[[s:1]][[s:3]]

What the October survey measured

Quinnipiac interviewed 1,159 likely voters from October 1 through October 5, 2026. Alongside Shapiro’s 59 percent and Garrity’s 34 percent, Quinnipiac reported Krawchuk at 2 percent, with 4 percent undecided and 1 percent refusing to answer. The reported margin of error is 3.9 percentage points, including the design effect.[[s:1]]

Those candidate figures put Shapiro 25 percentage points ahead of Garrity in this survey. The unit matters: that is the difference between their reported shares of respondents, rather than a market price or a statement about the eventual margin on election night.[[s:1]]

The same October 1–5 survey reported Shapiro’s job approval at 63 percent approving and 30 percent disapproving. Approval asks about his performance; the candidate-choice figures address a different question. Neither figure should stand in for the other.[[s:1]]

Why July is not a trend line

Quinnipiac calls the October release its first likely-voter poll of the cycle. Its July 9 to 13 survey of registered voters had Shapiro at 53 percent and Garrity at 40 percent, but the pollster says the surveys cannot be compared.[[s:6]][[s:1]] A different respondent population prevents treating the two sets of figures as a clean measure of movement.[[s:1]]

The useful finding is the October snapshot itself. October shows a 25-point difference and July a 13-point difference, but the pollster says the surveys cannot be compared.[[s:1]]

What the party contracts quote

At the November 1, 2026 capture, precisely 15:35:47 UTC, Kalshi’s GOVPARTYPA-26-D Democratic-party contract had a last-trade quote of 0.984, a Yes bid of 0.981 and a Yes ask of 0.984. The GOVPARTYPA-26-R Republican-party contract had a last quote of 0.022, a Yes bid of 0.018 and a Yes ask of 0.021.[[s:2]]

The labels display the candidates’ names, but the rules resolve on whether a representative of the specified party is inaugurated as governor pursuant to the 2026 election. These are party contracts, not contracts on an individual candidate. The quoted prices are not vote shares or calibrated win probabilities.[[s:2]]

What the TIP records can tell you

TIP shows that neither Shapiro nor Garrity has a congressional record and lists all six issues as “Not graded.” That is missing grading information, not a score of zero and not a finding that the candidates hold the same positions.[[s:4]][[s:5]]

The issue labels are Data Bill of Rights, War-Crime Accountability, Congressional Stock Trading Ban, Citizens United Reform, Universal Healthcare and US-Israel Military Merger. Naming those fields identifies the scope of the record; an ungraded field does not answer how either candidate would act on that issue.[[s:4]][[s:5]]

Read the Shapiro record and the Garrity record with that limit in view. A polling lead cannot fill an ungraded issue field, just as a contract price cannot establish a candidate’s policy commitments.[[s:4]][[s:5]][[s:1]][[s:2]]
