# Florida State of the Race: 90-second script and creative director's pitch

Version 1.0, 2026-10-24. Built from `FLORIDA_VIDEO_CONCEPT.md` (fact IDs F-01 to F-26 and their wording limits), the two published-ready articles and the share cards in `static/img/elections/social/`. Instagram, 9:16, 1080 x 1920, 30 fps, 90.0 seconds. Synthetic narrator (disclosed on the end card), the Artlist brass-techno bed, real public-domain portraits, deterministic data graphics, no synthetic footage.

**Arc, as the owner asked:** start general (the whole ballot), end on the Senate underdog story (the challenger who beat the money favorite and now trails by four), and finish on an action: early voting is open. Every line is a verified or attributed fact; the "underdog" feeling comes from the facts, never from a value word.

Pace: 5-second segments, 13 to 15 spoken words each (about 2.8 words a second), so 18 segments carry 230 words.

## 2. Continuous read (for the synthetic voice)
Florida votes November third, on thirty contests. What do the numbers actually show? One governor, one senator, twenty-eight House members. Eighty-three House candidates alone. First, the House. Florida redrew twenty-one of its twenty-eight districts this year. Reporters put Trump's 2024 vote at fifty-five to forty-four in Castor's new district. In August, Moskowitz, Fine and Wasserman Schultz all won primaries under the new lines. Each profile shows up to six issue grades. A dash means not graded. Governor: Donalds versus Jolly. Three numbers that do not agree. UNF's poll says forty-nine to forty-seven. Close, not a lead. Kalshi's Republican contract last traded at eighty-three cents. A price, not a vote share. Committee money, as of September eleventh: one hundred seven million, versus six. Not ad spending. A poll asks voters. A market shows traders. Money shows funding. None is a forecast. Now the Senate: a special election for the last two years of Rubio's term. Angie Nixon beat Alex Vindman, who had reported raising fourteen and a half million. The newest poll: Moody forty-nine, Nixon forty-five. Margin of error, nearly three. In July, one report says Moody led by eight. Now: four. Moody has declined to debate. She says she will keep engaging with voters. A challenger who beat the money favorite, four points back. Polls are snapshots. Early voting is open through October thirty-first. Every contest, every grade, linked below.

Voice direction: calm, close-miked, even pacing; drop half a tone on "not a forecast" and on "Polls are snapshots"; no theatrical emphasis on any candidate's name; both candidates in the Senate section get the same duration and the same screen size.

## 5. The creative director's pitch, five seconds at a time

**The one-paragraph case.** Most election videos pick a side or promise a prediction. This one makes a smaller, truer promise that is harder to ignore: *the numbers you keep seeing disagree, and here is why.* It starts as wide as possible so a Florida viewer sees their own ballot in the first ten seconds, it earns trust by labeling what every number cannot tell you, and it saves its emotional peak for the one story on the ballot with a built-in arc: a challenger who beat the money favorite and is four points back in the newest poll. It ends on something a viewer can do today, not on a conclusion. That order is the right choice because attention is won with the general and the surprising, trust is won in the middle, and enthusiasm is only durable if it points at voting rather than at a candidate.

| Segment | Why this is the right choice |
|---|---|
| **0:00 to 0:05** Hook: "thirty contests, what do the numbers actually show?" | A question in the first sentence opens a loop the viewer wants closed. "Thirty" is concrete and larger than anyone expects, so it signals value fast. Starting on the Old Capitol grounds it in Florida immediately. The title chip tells a scroller what the video is before the thumb moves. |
| **0:05 to 0:10** The 30-tile board | This is the screenshot people save: a one-frame map of the whole ballot. It proves the video is about everything, not one race, and it gives every viewer a tile that is theirs. Saves are the strongest quiet signal we can earn. |
| **0:10 to 0:15** "21 of 28 districts redrawn" | The most surprising fact in the dataset, stated as a count, not a motive. A count cannot be accused of spin, and the map filling teal makes it visible without words. Putting the House first keeps the video general before it narrows. |
| **0:15 to 0:20** The 55–44 and 58–41 bars | One visual idea (a split bar) that every viewer can read in a second. The "reporters' figures" label costs us nothing on screen and protects the whole video from the one number we did not compute ourselves. The courts line sits on screen so the narration stays short. |
| **0:20 to 0:25** Three primary winners, three portraits | Faces before names. Three quick pins on the map turn an abstract redraw into people and places, and it is the first appearance of real portraits, which reset attention at the 20-second mark where retention usually dips. |
| **0:25 to 0:30** The six issue slots and the dash | The brand moment, and the place where we teach the one rule that stops a viewer from misreading everything after: not graded is not zero. Teaching it early means the Senate section can show a grade without needing to explain it. |
| **0:30 to 0:35** "Governor: three numbers that do not agree" | This is the promise of the video made explicit. It is also the pivot from the general to the specific. Three empty chips on screen create a tiny scavenger hunt: the viewer wants to see them filled. |
| **0:35 to 0:40** UNF 49–47, "close, not a lead" | The plain-language gloss is the value we add over a headline. "Close, not a lead" is a sentence people can repeat, which is what makes a video shareable without being partisan. |
| **0:40 to 0:45** Kalshi at 83¢, "a price, not a vote share" | Prediction-market numbers are everywhere and almost always misread. A one-sentence correction is a public service and the most distinctive thing in the video. The cash-register tick gives the number a sound identity. |
| **0:45 to 0:50** $107.6M versus $6.4M | The most dramatic gap in the video, placed in the middle where the viewer has already been taught to distrust a single number. Seeing it with its caveat ("includes transfers, not ad spending") is what turns a shock into credibility. |
| **0:50 to 0:55** "A poll asks. A market shows. Money shows funding. None is a forecast." | The payoff of the open loop from segment 7, and the line most likely to be quoted in a comment. Resolving the loop at the 50-second mark rewards viewers who stayed, and it frees the Senate section to be pure story. |
| **0:55 to 1:00** Hard cut to the Senate | A hard cut, a new brass stab and a new card tell the brain a new chapter has started, which resets attention for the final third. "The last two years of Rubio's term" gives the stakes in a clause. |
| **1:00 to 1:05** Nixon beat Vindman, who had reported $14.5M | This is the underdog fact, stated as two verified facts side by side: a state representative won the primary, and the person she beat had reported far more money through June. We do not say she had less money, because the source does not give her figure. The viewer supplies the inference, which is stronger than ours would be. |
| **1:05 to 1:10** The poll: 49 and 45 | Portraits at equal size, bars at equal weight. The visual fairness is the editorial choice: the challenger and the incumbent get identical treatment, so the story reads as news, not advocacy. |
| **1:10 to 1:15** July 8 → now 4 | The motion beat. A gap marker sliding from eight to four is the single most "momentum" image in the video, and it is honest because the label says "one report". Momentum earned by a sourced comparison feels different from momentum asserted. |
| **1:15 to 1:20** The debate cards, equal size | This is the beat that keeps the ending credible. If the video ended on the challenger's good news only, a skeptical viewer would stop trusting the earlier fairness. Giving Moody's own words the same screen weight means the finale can run hot without being accused of tilting. |
| **1:20 to 1:25** "A challenger who beat the money favorite, four points back" | The peak: everything clears, two portraits, one sentence, the full brass drop. The line is a summary of three sourced facts, and it ends on "Polls are snapshots" so enthusiasm attaches to the fact that the race is open, not to a predicted result. |
| **1:25 to 1:30** Early voting is open; link in bio | The enthusiasm needs somewhere to go. "Open through October thirty-first" turns a feeling into an action with a deadline, and the link gives the saves and shares a destination (every contest, every grade). The credits and the synthetic-narration disclosure sit here so the trust we built is not undone by a missing label. |

**Why the order works as a whole.** General to specific is the retention curve: wide shots and surprising counts for the first 30 seconds, an explainer in the middle that makes the viewer feel smarter, a story at the end that makes them feel something. The Senate is the right finale because it is the only contest with a person-shaped arc and a number that moved, and because it is a single race, which lets the last 35 seconds slow down and breathe after 55 seconds of dense information.

**The risks, and how the script handles them.**
1. *The ending reads as favoring a candidate.* Controls: equal screen time and size for both candidates; every line attributed or sourced; the incumbent's own words get a full segment; no value words; the final sentence is about polls being snapshots and the next one is about voting, not about Nixon.
2. *A number goes stale before posting.* Segments 8, 9 and 10 are marked REFRESH and the script changes with the data; the freshness check (`tools/qa/freshness_check.py`) runs the morning of upload.
3. *Too dense for 90 seconds.* Each segment carries one number; if a cut runs long, drop segment 6 or 11 first, and never the board or the Senate finale.
4. *Single-source facts.* The July 8 and the committee totals are labeled on screen as single-source and dated; if either cannot be re-confirmed, replace segment 15 with the poll's undecided share and segment 10 with the matching-funds figures ($3.07M to Jolly, $1.20M to Donalds, state table Oct 23).
5. *No guarantee of reach.* This is built to be watched to the end, saved and sent; it is not a promise of virality. Upload the three hook variants from the concept document on different days and keep the one that holds the first three seconds and earns the most shares per reach.

**What I would ask the owner to approve before the storyboard:** the 83¢ and committee lines (they are the most striking and the most easily misread), the decision to end on the Senate challenger, and the end-card disclosure text.
