# Michigan's Final Five Days: Follow the Money
As of October 29, 2026. Single-host video podcast. Approximately 6 minutes with pauses.
Bracketed directions are production cues, not spoken copy. The connecting theme is editorial analysis; factual claims are attributed below.

[OPEN — host to camera; restrained music]
Five days before Michigan votes, a Senate candidate is facing religious smears, a DeVos-linked advertising push is testing campaign-finance limits and a group calling itself Progressive Champions is promoting a Green candidate with Republican-aligned money.

Meanwhile, voters could approve restrictions on utility influence, and a fight over a massive data center is bringing the argument about corporate power right into people's neighborhoods.

Six stories. One question running through them: who gets to shape Michigan's choices?

[POLLING — show the dated WDIV graphic]
Start with the Senate race.

A WDIV and Detroit News poll puts Abdul El-Sayed at 51.4 percent and Mike Rogers at 45.1 percent.

That's an advantage for El-Sayed. It's also a poll, with uncertainty attached: 600 likely voters, interviewed October 19 through 22, and a margin of error of four percentage points.

Those dates matter. The interviews happened before Trump's Sunday rally. This poll cannot tell us how voters reacted to that appearance.

The story to watch is whether El-Sayed can turn that reported advantage into actual votes, while Rogers tries to close the gap.

[WOVEN IN — approved line (old beat 8), over a plain dated FEC filing graphic]
Meanwhile, the United Democracy Project filed five notices opposing El-Sayed between October fifteenth and twenty-third, totaling about four hundred thousand dollars.

Sources: S1; filings line: S8.

[ATTACKS — rally photograph; return to host for analysis]
At that October 25 rally in Port Huron, Trump portrayed El-Sayed as a security threat and suggested he should face a metal detector before entering the Senate.

The Associated Press reports no evidence for the alleged Muslim Brotherhood affiliation.

That is a religious smear being deployed in a Senate campaign. Voters deserve to hear it identified clearly.

Even Bishara Bahbah, a founder of Arab Americans for Trump, condemned the remarks as racist.

My read: making a candidate's faith the focus of the closing days crowds out scrutiny of what either candidate would actually do in office.

Sources: S2.

[GOVERNOR'S RACE — John James photo; simple dollar figures]
Now follow the money into the governor's race.

Bridge Michigan reports a 1.8-million-dollar Michigan Republican Party advertising buy supporting John James, with funding linked to Dan and Douglas DeVos through a separate political committee.

Democrats filed a campaign-finance complaint.

Bridge reports that state law limits party assistance to 750,000 dollars for gubernatorial candidates who accepted public funding. James reported 1.9 million dollars in total party support.

James argues that a recent Supreme Court ruling on federal party spending supports his approach.

That dispute has not been resolved in the reporting we're citing.

The accountability question is about timing: if disputed spending helps shape an election now, what can a ruling delivered afterward actually repair?

Sources: S3.

[HOUSE MAILERS — actual flyer, then emphasize the payer disclosure]
In Michigan's Fourth Congressional District, a different money trail is hiding behind a very different name.

Mailers promote Green Party candidate Shafina Barnett over Democrat Sean McCann, emphasizing opposition to data centers.

The payer is Progressive Champions PAC.

But WMUK reports that all its funding came from Republican-aligned Conservative Americans PAC.

The Green Party says neither Barnett nor the party has any connection to these mailers. Its spokesperson calls the effort an attempt to pull votes away from Democrats.

Keep that distinction clear: this reporting concerns an outside group's activity. It does not establish that Barnett coordinated with Republicans.

For voters, the lesson is practical. Read the disclosure. Then check who funded the organization named in it.

Sources: S4.

[PROPOSAL 2 — petition photo; show the actual proposal title]
Michigan voters also have a direct decision about political money on their ballot.

Proposal Two would restrict contributions tied to regulated electric and gas utilities and certain government contractors. It would also expand disclosure rules for political communications.

A WDIV and Detroit News poll found 73.8 percent support after respondents heard the ballot summary.

Supporters connect the measure to frustration over power bills and outages: they want lawmakers freer to hold utilities accountable.

Opponents argue the restrictions infringe on political speech. Passage could bring legal challenges.

My read: the level of reported support makes this a major test of whether voters' frustration with corporate influence translates into a change in the rules.

Sources: S5 and S6.

[DATA CENTERS — protest photo; identify location of the proposed project in text]
And that frustration is showing up locally.

In Delhi Township, south of Lansing, officials have scheduled an October 30 meeting to consider formal opposition to data-center development.

That follows an announcement that developers are exploring a 423-acre site for a proposed 700-megawatt project, despite an existing township moratorium.

This is a proposal being explored, not an approved facility.

Developers describe benefits including a community fund, battery storage and closed-loop cooling. The township says it learned about the announcement when the media did and has ended its membership in the regional development organization involved.

The political question is who gets a meaningful say before a project advances.

And remember those Fourth District mailers: outside groups are already using data-center opposition as an election message.

Sources: S7; connection to mailers: S4.

[CLOSE — host to camera]
Watch the Senate vote. Watch the money behind the governor's ads. Read the fine print on the mailers. Then look at Proposal Two and the decisions being made in township halls.

These stories connect national campaign tactics with the power people experience close to home.

Election Day is November third. Our source links are below.
